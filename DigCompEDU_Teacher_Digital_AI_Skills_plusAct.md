## 1 · Digital Competences (DigCompEdu 2017)

DigCompEdu organises educator digital competences across **22 elementary competences** in
**six areas**, spanning educators' own professional practice through to learners' digital
competence development. Areas 1 and 6 bracket the model: professional engagement on one
side, enabling learner competence on the other; areas 2–5 cover educators' pedagogic
competences.

| Area | Focus | Sub-competences |
|---|---|---|
| **1 · Professional Engagement** | Digital use in professional interaction and own development | 1.1 Organisational communication; 1.2 Professional collaboration; 1.3 Reflective practice; 1.4 Digital CPD |
| **2 · Digital Resources** | Responsible creation, use, and sharing of digital learning resources | 2.1 Selecting; 2.2 Creating & modifying; 2.3 Managing, protecting, sharing |
| **3 · Teaching and Learning** | Managing and orchestrating digital technologies in teaching | 3.1 Teaching; 3.2 Guidance; 3.3 Collaborative learning; 3.4 Self-regulated learning |
| **4 · Assessment** | Incorporating digital strategies to enhance assessment | 4.1 Assessment strategies; 4.2 Analysing evidence; 4.3 Feedback & planning |
| **5 · Empowering Learners** | Learner-centred digital teaching and differentiation | 5.1 Accessibility & inclusion; 5.2 Differentiation & personalisation; 5.3 Actively engaging learners |
| **6 · Facilitating Learners' Digital Competence** | Pedagogic development of students' own digital skills | 6.1 Information & media literacy; 6.2 Communication; 6.3 Content creation; 6.4 Responsible use; 6.5 Problem solving |

### Progression model

Six stages apply uniformly across all 22 competences:

**Newcomer (A1) → Explorer (A2) → Integrator (B1) → Expert (B2) → Leader (C1) → Pioneer (C2)**

The model describes development from *assimilating basic digital practices* through
*integrating and experimenting* to *critiquing, innovating, and influencing policy*.

## 2 · AI-Specific Competences (DigCompEdu Supplement 2023)

The Supplement extends each of the six DigCompEdu areas with AI-specific competences,
retaining the same six-stage progression. For each area the Supplement specifies activities,
proficiency statements, and key challenges.

### 2.1 Professional Engagement + AI

- Use AI-driven platforms for personalised professional development and continuous learning
- Leverage AI-powered communication tools to streamline administrative tasks and focus on meaningful interaction
- Participate in AI-focused professional learning communities; share experiences and best practices
- Apply AI analytics to educational data to support evidence-based institutional decision-making
- Engage critically with ethical dimensions of AI in professional practice: data privacy, algorithmic bias, equitable use
- Contribute to institutional AI policy development and advocate for responsible use

### 2.2 Digital Resources + AI

- Create adaptive, personalised learning materials using AI tools (adaptive textbooks, AI-generated content, simulations)
- Critically evaluate pedagogical quality, accuracy, and relevance of AI-generated content
- Use AI-powered search and recommendation systems to efficiently curate resources from digital libraries
- Automate detection of copyright issues and manage digital rights; ensure privacy compliance
- Share and discuss AI-enhanced resources responsibly within professional communities

### 2.3 Teaching and Learning + AI

- Integrate AI-driven adaptive learning systems that adjust difficulty and content to individual learning needs
- Deploy intelligent tutoring systems and conversational AI tools for individualised student support
- Design interactive, AI-enhanced learning experiences: simulations, games, NLP-based conversational interfaces
- Use AI analytics to monitor student progress and identify intervention needs
- Make evidence-based decisions about when to use AI tools and when human interaction is preferable
- Balance AI-driven instruction with traditional methods to cater to varied learning contexts

### 2.4 Assessment + AI

- Implement AI-assisted automated grading for objective assessments and written responses
- Use AI-driven analytics to identify patterns, learning gaps, and areas requiring targeted intervention
- Design adaptive tests that adjust question difficulty to individual learner ability in real time
- Provide timely, personalised AI-generated feedback while retaining final pedagogical judgement
- Develop innovative AI-based assessment formats (game-based, simulation-based, project-based)
- Ensure fairness, transparency, academic integrity, and data privacy in all AI-assisted assessment

### 2.5 Empowering Learners + AI

- Deploy AI adaptive learning platforms tailored to diverse learning needs and styles
- Use AI accessibility tools — speech recognition, language translation, visual aids — for inclusive education
- Foster learner autonomy and self-regulated learning through AI-powered resources
- Guide learners to engage critically with AI's capabilities, limitations, and societal impact
- Prepare students for AI-integrated workplaces; develop data literacy alongside digital skills

### 2.6 Facilitating Learners' Digital Competence + AI

- Develop and deliver AI literacy curricula covering AI concepts, applications, and societal role
- Teach ethical AI use: data privacy, algorithmic bias, digital citizenship, responsible interaction
- Guide learners to critically assess AI-generated information and to identify AI-generated disinformation
- Facilitate creative and interdisciplinary AI projects (coding, data analysis, content creation, simulations)
- Support learners in understanding how AI systems work, their limitations, and their role in society

## 3 · Transversal Skills for Educators (Supplement, Chapter 6)

The Supplement identifies transversal skills that underpin effective AI integration across
all six areas. These complement technical competences and are essential for navigating
the dynamic nature of AI in education.

| Skill cluster | What it covers |
|---|---|
| **Digital literacy** | Proficiency with AI tools; navigating AI-enhanced platforms |
| **Critical thinking** | Evaluating AI outputs; identifying bias in algorithms |
| **Problem-solving** | Using AI for innovative educational solutions; adapting tools to new contexts |
| **Ethical awareness** | Privacy, data security, equity; promoting responsible AI use among students |
| **Lifelong learning** | Continuous upskilling in a rapidly evolving AI landscape |
| **Communication** | Explaining complex AI concepts to learners and stakeholders |
| **Collaboration** | Working with peers, IT staff, and industry on AI-related projects |
| **Creativity & innovation** | Incorporating AI in creative teaching strategies; fostering student creativity |
| **Emotional intelligence** | Recognising social-emotional implications of AI for learners; providing support |
| **Adaptability** | Flexibility to experiment with and adopt evolving AI applications |

## 4 · EU AI Act — Bridging Document

> ⚖️ **Status note:** The EU AI Act (Regulation 2024/1689, in force from 1 August 2024,
> with staggered application through 2027) is **not** a competence or training framework.
> It is binding EU law. It does not prescribe what teachers must *know* or *be able to do*
> in the sense of a framework; it prescribes what AI systems may or may not be used for,
> and what obligations apply to anyone who deploys them — including educational institutions.
> Its relevance here is as the **legal floor** beneath any competence discussion: framework
> competences tell teachers *what good looks like*; the EU AI Act tells them *what is
> required or forbidden by law*.

### 4.1 How the Act defines "AI literacy" (Art. 3 § 56)

The Act defines *AI literacy* as:

> skills, knowledge and understanding that allow providers, deployers and affected persons,
> taking into account their respective rights and obligations in the context of this
> Regulation, to make an informed deployment of AI systems, as well as to gain awareness
> about the opportunities and risks of AI and possible harm it can cause.

This is a narrower, rights-and-obligations-centred definition than the competence
frameworks use, but it provides the legal anchor for any AI literacy requirement in
educational institutions acting as AI *deployers*.

### 4.2 AI literacy obligation for deployers (Art. 4)

Providers **and deployers** of AI systems — this includes schools and universities — must
take measures to ensure, to the best of their ability, *a sufficient level of AI literacy
of their staff and other persons dealing with the operation and use of AI systems on their
behalf*, taking into account their technical knowledge, experience, education, training,
and the context in which the AI systems are used.

**Implication for teachers:** AI literacy is not merely desirable professional development
— it is a legal obligation on the *institution*, and by extension a professional
responsibility of the staff it deploys to operate AI tools.

### 4.3 Prohibited AI practices in educational settings (Art. 5)

Two prohibitions in Article 5 apply directly in school and university contexts:

**Art. 5(1)(b) — Exploitation of vulnerabilities**
Prohibited: AI systems that exploit vulnerabilities of persons due to **age** (or disability,
or social/economic situation) with the effect of materially distorting their behaviour in
a harmful way. Children and young people in educational settings are an explicitly
contemplated protected group in the Recitals.

**Art. 5(1)(f) — Emotion recognition in education**
Prohibited: AI systems that **infer the emotions of natural persons in educational
institutions**, except where the use is intended for medical or safety reasons. This rules
out affective computing tools (e.g. engagement or attention monitoring via facial
expressions) used for instructional or administrative purposes in schools.

### 4.4 High-risk AI systems in education (Annex III, point 3)

The following categories are classified as **high-risk** AI systems, triggering the full
set of provider and deployer obligations (transparency, human oversight, fundamental rights
impact assessment, registration in the EU database, etc.):

| Sub-category | What it covers |
|---|---|
| **3(a)** | AI systems to determine **access or admission** to educational and vocational training institutions at all levels |
| **3(b)** | AI systems to **evaluate learning outcomes**, including where used to steer individual learning paths |
| **3(c)** | AI systems to **assess the appropriate level of education** an individual will receive or be able to access |
| **3(d)** | AI systems to **monitor and detect prohibited behaviour** of students during tests |

*Recital 56* explains the rationale: these systems can determine the educational and
professional course of a person's life and may violate the right to education, the right
not to be discriminated against, and may perpetuate historical discrimination patterns.

### 4.5 Human oversight obligations (Art. 14 + Art. 26)

When an educational institution deploys a high-risk AI system, it must ensure that the
assigned human overseer is able to:

- **understand** the capacities and limitations of the system and monitor its operation
- **detect** anomalies, dysfunctions, and unexpected performance
- **resist automation bias** — the tendency to over-rely on AI outputs for decisions
- **correctly interpret** the system's output using available tools and methods
- **override or disregard** the output in any specific situation
- **intervene or stop** the system through a safe-halt procedure

Additionally, schools and universities as deployers (Art. 26) must:

- assign oversight to persons with the **necessary competence, training and authority**
- **monitor** the AI system's operation according to the instructions for use
- **inform learners** when they are subject to a high-risk AI system (Art. 26 § 11)
- for public bodies: perform a **fundamental rights impact assessment** (Art. 27) before
  deploying Annex III high-risk systems

### 4.6 Mapping EU AI Act obligations to DigCompEdu areas

The Act does not map to competence areas by design, but its obligations translate into
teacher competence requirements as follows:

| EU AI Act provision | Most relevant DigCompEdu area |
|---|---|
| Art. 4 — AI literacy obligation | Area 1 (Professional Engagement / Digital CPD) |
| Art. 5(1)(f) — emotion recognition ban | Area 3 (Teaching & Learning) + Area 4 (Assessment) |
| Annex III 3(b/c) — high-risk assessment AI | Area 4 (Assessment) |
| Art. 14 — human oversight competence | Areas 3, 4, 5 (all pedagogic areas) |
| Art. 26 § 11 — inform learners | Area 5 (Empowering Learners) |
| Fundamental rights impact (Art. 27) | Area 1 (Professional Engagement) |
| General transparency & bias (Recital 56) | Area 6 (Facilitating Learners' Digital Competence) |

## 6 · Reference Framework

DigCompEdu is designed as a **universally applicable reference** for developers of digital
competence models at Member State, regional, institutional, and professional training
levels. It covers all educational levels from early childhood to higher and adult education,
including general, vocational, and non-formal contexts.

The **AI Pioneers Supplement** was developed under an ERASMUS+ Forward Looking Project
(2023) coordinated by the Institute for Technology and Education (ITB), University of
Bremen, with partners across Greece, Portugal, Italy, Spain, Estonia, Cyprus, and Germany
(BIBB). It retained the DigCompEdu six-area structure and Newcomer-to-Pioneer progression
model to ensure integration with existing training programmes.

The **EU AI Act** (Regulation 2024/1689) entered into force 1 August 2024. Key obligations
are phased in: prohibited practices apply from February 2025; high-risk system requirements
for Annex III systems (including education) apply from August 2026. Member States must
ensure market surveillance and penalties are in place. The Act is also cited as reference
in the KMK 2024 *Handlungsempfehlung*.
